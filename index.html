An improved bound for the exceptional set of the Erdős–Straus equation

Sushaan Kandukoori, Shrikrishna Ramesh, Pranava Kumar

Paper Lean code BibTeX

Can \(4/n\) always be split into three unit fractions?

Take any integer \(n\ge2\). Erdős and Straus conjectured that there are always positive integers \(x,y,z\) with

\[ \frac4n=\frac1x+\frac1y+\frac1z. \]

Try a few numbers in the box. It keeps working, and nobody has proved that it always will. What can be proved is that exceptions are rare.

Let \(E(N)\) count the \(n\le N\) with no solution. Vaughan showed in 1970 that \(E(N)\ll N\exp\{-c(\log N)^{2/3}\}\). We raise the exponent.

Theorem 1. There is an absolute constant \(c>0\) such that, for all \(N\ge3\), \[ E(N)\ll N\exp\{-c(\log N)^{3/4}\}. \]

The same saving holds for exceptional primes, measured against \(\pi(N)\). It also holds for \(m/n\) with any fixed numerator \(m\ge4\), with constants depending on \(m\). (For \(m=2,3\) there is nothing to prove: \(\tfrac2n=\tfrac1n+\tfrac1{2n}+\tfrac1{2n}\) and \(\tfrac3n=\tfrac1n+\tfrac1n+\tfrac1n\).)

Find a solution

Uses the congruence explained in the next section. Each answer is checked with exact integer arithmetic.

One divisor, one congruence, three fractions

Here is the engine. Fix \(a\ge1\), pick a divisor \(h\) of \(a^2\), and suppose that \(4a-1\) divides \(n+4h\). Then

\[ x=an,\qquad y=\frac{an+h}{4a-1},\qquad z=\frac{an+(an)^2/h}{4a-1} \]

are positive integers, and they solve the equation. The algebra is short. Write \(A=an\) and \(q=4a-1\). Then \(qy-A=h\) and \(qz-A=A^2/h\), and these multiply to give \((qy-A)(qz-A)=A^2\). Expanding gives \(q=A(1/y+1/z)\), so

\[ \frac1x+\frac1y+\frac1z=\frac{1+q}{an}=\frac4n. \]

For example, \(n=2521\), \(a=12\), \(h=16\): here \(47\) divides \(2521+64=2585=47\cdot55\), which gives

\[ \frac4{2521}=\frac1{644}+\frac1{30252}+\frac1{1217643}. \]

So each \(a\) supplies one class of \(n\) modulo \(4a-1\) for every divisor of \(a^2\). The choices \(h=1,a,a^2\) are the simplest, with shifts \(-4,-1,-a\). We call the rest the interior divisors. Congruences of this kind are classical. The new saving comes from using the interior divisors with moduli \(4a-1\) that need not be prime.

What the interior divisors buy

Run the congruences as a sieve. Take the integers from \(2\) to \(10^6\) and, as \(a\) grows, cross off every class it supplies.

With only the three divisors \(h=1,a,a^2\), 60,843 integers remain when \(a\) reaches 1,000. With every divisor, 1,002 remain. This picture only illustrates small numbers, and the proof uses a much thinner family, but it shows how much the interior divisors add.

Of the 1,002 survivors, 999 are perfect squares, and the other three are 288, 336 and 4545. No congruence of this kind can ever cross off a perfect square.

Why squares are never crossed off

Suppose \(k^2\equiv-4h\pmod q\), where \(q=4a-1\) and \(h\mid a^2\). Write \(h=d^2f\) with \(a=dfA\), so \(f\mid a\). Since \(q\equiv3\pmod4\), the congruence forces the Jacobi symbol \((f/q)\) to equal \(-1\).

But reciprocity and \(q\equiv-1\pmod g\) give \((g/q)=1\) for the odd part \(g\) of \(f\). If \(f\) is even, then \(a\) is even and \(q\equiv-1\pmod8\), so \((2/q)=1\). Hence \((f/q)=1\), a contradiction.

This does not threaten the theorem. There are at most \(\sqrt N\) squares up to \(N\), and \(k^2\) inherits a solution from any divisor of \(k\) that has one: scale the denominators.

Where the cube comes from

Measure the weight of the family by adding up the densities \(1/(4a-1)\) of its classes. A pair \((a,h)\) with \(h\mid a^2\) is a triple in disguise. Put \(g=(a,h)\), \(d=h/g\), \(f=g/d\) and \(A=a/g\). Then

\[ a=dfA,\qquad h=d^2f,\qquad (d,A)=1, \]

and the triple is unique. So the weight is about \(\tfrac14\sum 1/(dfA)\) over \(dfA\le L\), a harmonic sum in three free variables, which has size \((\log L)^3\). The coprimality condition costs a factor \(1/\zeta(2)\), and the three-dimensional simplex costs \(1/6\):

\[ \sum_{a\le L}\frac{|\Lambda_{\mathrm{int}}(a)|}{4a-1}\sim\frac{(\log L)^3}{24\,\zeta(2)}, \]

where \(\Lambda_{\mathrm{int}}(a)\) is the set of interior divisors of \(a^2\).

There is a catch. Congruences with coprime moduli are independent of each other, by the Chinese remainder theorem, so pairwise coprime moduli \(4a-1\) would be the easy case. But if the integers \(4a-1\) are pairwise coprime, the same sum is only \(O((\log L)^2)\). Restricting \(4a-1\) to primes is one such case, and it is the lower curve in the chart. The extra logarithm comes from moduli that share factors, so the overlaps have to be faced.

The weight, all \(a\) against \(4a-1\) prime

\(\sum_{a\le L}|\Lambda_{\mathrm{int}}(a)|/(4a-1)\), computed up to \(L=2\cdot10^6\). At this range the lower-order terms are still large: the upper curve is about 1.5 times its main term.

Why a cube means \(3/4\)

The exponent comes from a trade-off that is easy to state. Suppose a family of congruences has moduli at most \(X\) and total weight \(\mu\asymp(\log X)^k\).

The reward

If the classes behave nearly independently, the proportion of integers missed by all of them is about \(e^{-\mu}\). More weight means a bigger saving.

The price

Seeing this on integers up to \(N\) takes inclusion–exclusion to depth of order \(\mu\), with moduli up to \(X^{O(\mu)}\). We also fix a class modulo \(P(z)\), the product of the primes up to \(z\). All of this must fit: \(\log P(z)+O(\mu\log X)\le\log N\).

The balance

With \(\log P(z)=O((\log X)^{k+1})\), both costs have size \((\log X)^{k+1}\). So take \(\log X\) of order \((\log N)^{1/(k+1)}\). The saving is then \(\exp\{-c(\log N)^{k/(k+1)}\}\).

A family that keeps the cube

Fix \(m\ge4\), put \(z=(\log X)^4\), and fix a reduced class \(b\) modulo \(P(z)\). Each congruence in the family is built from four pieces, and each piece accounts for one factor in the weight.

The divisor \(e=rs^2\)

Here \(r,s\) are squarefree and coprime. We keep \(s\) small, at most \(X^{1/100}\), and let \(e\) run up to \(X^{3/10}\) with \(rs\) above \(X^{1/10}\). The sum over \(r\) and the sum over \(s\) each give a logarithm.

Gives \((\log X)^2\)

The small part \(d_-\)

An odd squarefree divisor of \(P(z)\), at most \((\log X)^8\), with \(b\equiv-me\pmod{d_-}\). The fixed class \(b\) already takes care of this part of the congruence, so \(d_-\) does not count against the density of the class. Only some \(e\) pass the test, but averaging over \(e\) leaves, up to constants, the sum of \(1/d_-\), which has size \(\log z\).

Gives \(\log z\)

The rough part \(d_+\)

Squarefree with no prime factor up to \(z\), and small: \(d_-d_+\le X^{1/20}\). Numbers with no prime factor up to \(z\) have density about \(1/\log z\), so the sum of \(1/d_+\) has size about \(\log X/\log z\).

Gives \(\log X/\log z\)

The large prime \(p\)

A prime with \(X^{2/3}<p\le X^{19/20}\) and \(d_-d_+p\equiv-1\pmod{m\operatorname{rad}(e)}\), where \(\operatorname{rad}(e)=rs\). The Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem says that primes spread evenly among residue classes, on average over moduli up to about the square root of their size. It lets us count these primes.

Gives a positive constant

\( (\log X)^2\cdot\log z\cdot\dfrac{\log X}{\log z}\asymp(\log X)^3 \)

The \(\log z\) cancels, so removing the small primes from the rough part costs nothing overall. The result is \(\mu_b\asymp_m(\log X)^3\), uniformly in \(b\). Every member of the family does produce solutions. The condition on \(p\) makes \(a=(d_-d_+p+1)/m\) an integer divisible by \(\operatorname{rad}(e)\), so \(e\mid a^2\). The congruence from the second section, with \(4\) replaced by \(m\), then gives a representation for every \(n\equiv-me\) modulo \(d_-d_+p\).

Almost independent is enough

After fixing \(n\equiv b\pmod{P(z)}\), each congruence is an event of probability \(1/q\) with \(q=d_+p\). Independent events would all be missed with probability about \(e^{-\mu}\). Two facts show that these events are close enough to independent.

Compatible events cannot share their large prime

Call two events compatible if some integer satisfies both. Suppose two compatible events have the same large prime \(p\), with divisors \(e\) and \(e'\). Then \(me\equiv me'\pmod p\), and \(e,e'\) are much smaller than \(p\), so \(e=e'\). The condition on \(p\) then fixes \(d_-d_+\) modulo \(m\operatorname{rad}(e)\). That product is smaller than \(\operatorname{rad}(e)\), so it is fixed outright, and the two events are the same.

Everything else is a rough common factor

So dependent events share only primes above \(z\), and taking \(z\) as large as \((\log X)^4\) makes those primes rare. Call the events that share one with a given event its neighbours. Their total weight is at most about

\[ \mu_b\cdot\frac{\log X}{z\log z}\ll\frac1{\log\log X}. \]

Writing \(\delta_b\) for this neighbour weight and \(\Delta_b\) for the sum of the pair probabilities over dependent pairs, we get \(\delta_b=o(1)\) and \(\Delta_b=o(\mu_b)\).

Theorem 5.2 of the paper, with the inequality from its proof shown. If \(W_b\) counts the events that occur, \(\delta_b\le\tfrac14\) and \(\Delta_b=o(\mu_b)\), then \[ \mathbb P(W_b=0)\le\exp\left\{-\mu_b+\frac{\Delta_b}{1-2\delta_b}\right\}=\exp\{-(1-o(1))\mu_b\}. \]

The proof is a short conditioning argument. By induction, conditioning on any earlier events not occurring at most doubles the probability of an event. That bound shows the conditional probability is at least \(1/q\) minus the pair terms divided by \(1-2\delta_b\). Multiplying along an ordering of the events gives the bound, so no correlation inequality has to be quoted.

From the model to actual integers

So far \(n\) has been random modulo each prime. To pass to actual integers, expand the chance of missing every event by inclusion–exclusion and stop at the even depth \(r=2\lfloor13\mu_b\rfloor\). What is left out is below \(e^{-4\mu_b}\), and each intersection of events is counted correctly to within \(1\). Those errors stay small as long as

\[ P(z)X^{r}e^{2\mu_b}\le Ne^{-4\mu_b}. \]

Finishing

Take \(\log X=\alpha(\log N)^{1/4}\) with \(\alpha\) small. Then \(\mu_b\) has order \((\log N)^{3/4}\) and the condition above holds. This bounds the exceptions coprime to \(P(z)\). Exceptions are closed under taking divisors, because a representation for a divisor of \(n\) scales up to \(n\). So the integers with small prime factors follow by pulling those factors out, and the bound holds for all \(n\).

What remains open

The conjecture itself. The theorem says exceptions are very rare, and it does not rule them out.

The proof also gives no computable values for its thresholds, because the usual Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem does not supply them. Applying effective variants of that theorem to this family would need a separate analysis.

For the full statements and proofs, with every hypothesis and constant, read the paper.
