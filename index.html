An improved bound for the exceptional set of the Erdős–Straus equation

Sushaan Kandukoori, Shrikrishna Ramesh, Pranava Kumar
Paper Code BibTeX

Abstract

Let \(E(N)\) be the number of integers \(2\le n\le N\) for which

\[ \frac4n=\frac1x+\frac1y+\frac1z \]

has no solution in positive integers. Vaughan proved \(E(N)\ll N\exp\{-c(\log N)^{2/3}\}\). We prove

\[ E(N)\ll N\exp\{-c(\log N)^{3/4}\}. \]

For each \(a\ge1\), the divisors of \(a^2\) supply congruence classes modulo \(4a-1\) on which such representations exist. For a large parameter \(X\), we select a family whose reciprocal moduli sum to order \((\log X)^3\). Averaging over small divisors retains this weight while controlling shared prime factors. A finite counting argument gives the stated bound at the scale \(\log X\asymp(\log N)^{1/4}\). The same argument gives this exponent for every fixed numerator \(m\ge4\).

1 Introduction and main theorem

The Erdős–Straus conjecture asserts that for every integer \(n\ge2\) the equation

\[ \frac4n=\frac1x+\frac1y+\frac1z \]

has a solution in positive integers. Let \(E(N)\) count the exceptions \(2\le n\le N\). Vaughan proved the classical estimate

\[ E(N)\ll N\exp\{-c(\log N)^{2/3}\} \]

for some absolute \(c>0\). Our main result improves the exponent.

Theorem 1 (main theorem). There is an absolute constant \(c>0\) such that, for all \(N\ge3\), \[ E(N)\ll N\exp\{-c(\log N)^{3/4}\}. \]

The same saving holds relative to the primes: the number of exceptional primes up to \(N\) is \(\ll\pi(N)\exp\{-c(\log N)^{3/4}\}\) after decreasing \(c\).

For \(m\ge4\), let \(E_m(N)\) count the positive integers \(n\le N\) for which \(m/n\) is not a sum of three unit fractions.

Theorem 2 (Erdős–Straus–Schinzel exceptional sets for a fixed numerator). For each fixed integer \(m\ge4\), there are constants \(c_m,C_m>0\) such that, for all \(N\ge3\), \[ E_m(N)\le C_mN\exp\{-c_m(\log N)^{3/4}\}. \]

The cases \(m=2,3\) have no exceptions, since

\[ \frac2n=\frac1n+\frac1{2n}+\frac1{2n},\qquad \frac3n=\frac1n+\frac1n+\frac1n. \]
Example 1.1. For \(n=2521\), take \(a=12\) and \(h=16\). Then \(h\mid a^2\) and \[ 4a-1=47,\qquad n+4h=2585=47\cdot55. \] Lemma 2.3 gives \[ \frac4{2521}=\frac1{644}+\frac1{30252}+\frac1{1217643}. \]

We use the Type II congruences

\[ 4a-1\mid n+4h,\qquad h\mid a^2. \]

The choices \(h=1,a,a^2\) give the shifts \(-4,-1,-a\) modulo \(4a-1\), respectively. The sum over the remaining divisors has cubic logarithmic order; see Proposition 4.2.

In the selected family, compatible congruences with the same large prime coincide. We bound the remaining overlaps by sums over common small factors. Two divisor sums give weight \((\log X)^2\); averaging over small divisors supplies \(\log z\), which combines with the rough cofactor sum \(\log X/\log z\) to give the third logarithm.

Remark 1.2 (the exponent 3/4). For a family of congruences with total weight \(\mu\asymp(\log X)^k\) and the overlap estimates below, the level condition of Theorem 5.4 has the form \(\log P(z)+O(\mu\log X)\le\log N\). When \(\log P(z)=O((\log X)^{k+1})\), choosing \(\log X\) of order \((\log N)^{1/(k+1)}\) gives a saving \(\exp\{-c(\log N)^{k/(k+1)}\}\). The family with prime moduli used in the Vaughan method has quadratic mass. Here the cubic mass gives \(3/4\).

2 Congruences

Fix \(m\ge4\). A Type II representation for a prime denominator is one in which exactly two denominators are divisible by the prime. The proof of Theorem 1 uses only Lemma 2.3.

Theorem 2.1 (Type II representations). Let \(p\) be a prime. Suppose that positive integers \(d,f,A,B\) satisfy \[ (mdfA-1)B=Ap+d. \tag{2} \] Then \[ \frac m p=\frac1{pdfA}+\frac1{pfAB}+\frac1{dfB}. \] Conversely, every Type II representation can be put in this form after permuting the two denominators divisible by \(p\).

Put \(a=dfA\) and \(h=d^2f\). Then \(h\mid a^2\).

Theorem 2.2 (Type II congruences). Let \(p\) be a prime and \(a\ge1\). There is a Type II solution with first denominator \(pa\) if and only if there exists a divisor \(h\mid a^2\) such that \[ ma-1\mid p+mh. \tag{3} \] When (3) holds, write \(h=d^2f\) and \(a=dfA\). Then \[ B=\frac{Ap+d}{mdfA-1} \] is integral, and Theorem 2.1 gives a Type II representation.

For arbitrary positive denominators we use the following sufficient condition.

Lemma 2.3 (a sufficient condition). Let \(n\ge1\), let \(a\ge1\), let \(q=ma-1\), and let \(e\) be a positive divisor of \(a^2\). If \[ n\equiv-me\pmod q, \] then \(m/n\) is a sum of three positive unit fractions.

Proof. Put \(A=an\). Since \(ma\equiv1\pmod q\), the congruence on \(n\) gives \(A\equiv-e\pmod q\). Since \(e\mid a^2\) and \((a,q)=1\), also \((e,q)=1\). Define

\[ x=A,\qquad y=\frac{A+e}{q},\qquad z=\frac{A+A^2/e}{q}. \]

Since \(e\mid a^2\), the quantity \(A^2/e=(a^2/e)n^2\) is an integer. The congruence \(A\equiv-e\pmod q\) makes \(y\) integral; squaring and multiplying by the inverse of \(e\) modulo \(q\) gives \(A^2/e\equiv e\pmod q\), so \(z\) is integral too. Both are positive. Moreover

\[ qy-A=e,\qquad qz-A=A^2/e, \]

and hence \((qy-A)(qz-A)=A^2\). Expanding and cancelling \(A^2\) gives

\[ q^2yz-qA(y+z)=0. \]

Dividing by \(qyz\) yields \(q=A(1/y+1/z)\). Therefore

\[ \frac1x+\frac1y+\frac1z=\frac{1+q}{an}=\frac m n. \]

3 Preliminary estimates

Fix

(4)\[ (\eta,\sigma,\lambda,\theta,\beta,\nu)=\left(\frac1{100},\frac1{20},\frac1{10},\frac3{10},\frac23,\frac8{25}\right). \]

Thus \(3\eta<\lambda\), \(\eta+\sigma<\lambda\), \(\lambda+\eta<\theta\), and \(\theta<\nu<\beta/2<\beta<1-\sigma\). Put

\[ z=(\log X)^4,\qquad U=(\log X)^8,\qquad P(z)=\prod_{\ell\le z}\ell. \]

The product is over primes.

The family in Section 6 is constructed as follows. Choose \(e=rs^2\) with \(r,s\) squarefree and coprime, a small divisor \(d_-\mid P(z)\), a squarefree cofactor \(d_+\) with \((d_+,P(z))=1\), and a prime \(p\). Require \(d_-d_+p\equiv-1\pmod{m\operatorname{rad}(e)}\). Then \(a=(d_-d_+p+1)/m\) satisfies \(e\mid a^2\), and Lemma 2.3 supplies the class \(n\equiv-me\pmod{d_-d_+p}\). After fixing \(n\equiv b\pmod{P(z)}\), require \(b\equiv-me\pmod{d_-}\); the remaining modulus is \(d_+p\). Section 5 gives the counting argument for these classes.

Lemma 3.4 (rough reciprocal sums over an initial segment). Let \(0<a_0\le b_0\) and \(C_0>0\) be fixed. Uniformly for \(X^{a_0}\le x\le X^{b_0}\) and \(1\le M\le X^{C_0}\), \[ \sum_{\substack{n\le x\\n\ \mathrm{squarefree}\\(n,P(z))=1\\(n,M)=1}}\frac1n\asymp_{a_0,b_0,C_0}\frac{\log X}{\log z}. \]
Theorem 3.7 (Bombieri–Vinogradov). For every fixed \(A>0\) and \(0<\vartheta<1/2\), uniformly for \(x\ge3\), \[ \sum_{q\le x^\vartheta}\max_{(a,q)=1}\left|\pi(x;q,a)-\frac{\pi(x)}{\varphi(q)}\right|\ll_{A,\vartheta}\frac{x}{(\log x)^A}. \]
Lemma 3.8 (reciprocal primes in progressions). Put \[ \mathcal P_X=\sum_{X^\beta<p\le X^{1-\sigma}}\frac1p,\qquad G_q(a)=\sum_{\substack{X^\beta<p\le X^{1-\sigma}\\p\equiv a\pmod q}}\frac1p-\frac{\mathcal P_X}{\varphi(q)}. \] For the exponents in (4), \[ \sum_{q\le X^\nu}\tau(q)\max_{(a,q)=1}|G_q(a)|\ll(\log X)^{-7}. \]

4 Cubic divisor sums and pairwise coprimality

This section concerns the numerator \(4\). The proof of Theorem 1 uses Sections 2, 3, 5, and 6. For \(a\ge1\), set

\[ \Lambda_{\mathrm{int}}(a)=\{h:h\mid a^2,\ h\notin\{1,a,a^2\}\},\qquad \Lambda_{<}(a)=\{h:h\mid a^2,\ 1<h<a\}. \]
Lemma 4.1 (a divisor identity). Writing \(\tau_3(n)\) for the number of ordered triples of factors of \(n\), we have \[ \tau(n^2)=\sum_{k^2\mid n}\mu(k)\tau_3(n/k^2). \] Consequently, \[ \sum_{n\le L}\frac{\tau(n^2)}n=\frac1{6\zeta(2)}(\log L)^3+O((\log L)^2). \]

Proof. Both sides of the identity are multiplicative. At \(n=p^j\), it reads \(\binom{j+2}{2}-\binom j2=2j+1\), with the second term omitted for \(j<2\).

For later use, fix \(M\).

(9)\[ \sum_{\substack{xyz\le T\\x\equiv r_1,\ y\equiv r_2,\ z\equiv r_3\pmod M}}\frac1{xyz}=\frac{(\log T)^3}{6M^3}+O_M((1+\log T)^2). \]

With \(M=1\), the divisor identity now yields

\[ \sum_{n\le L}\frac{\tau(n^2)}n=\frac16\sum_{k\le\sqrt L}\frac{\mu(k)}{k^2}\{\log(L/k^2)\}^3+O((1+\log L)^2). \]

The completed sum is \(\sum_k\mu(k)/k^2=1/\zeta(2)\).

Proposition 4.2 (cubic divisor sum). As \(L\to\infty\), \[ \sum_{a\le L}\frac{|\Lambda_{\mathrm{int}}(a)|}{4a-1}\sim\frac1{24\zeta(2)}(\log L)^3. \] Moreover, \[ \sum_{a\le L}\frac{|\Lambda_{<}(a)|}{4a-1}\sim\frac1{48\zeta(2)}(\log L)^3. \]

Proof. The involution \(h\mapsto a^2/h\) pairs divisors below and above \(a\). The excluded divisors \(1,a,a^2\) contribute only \(O(\log L)\) to the weighted sum. The preceding lemma also gives \(\sum_{a\ge1}\tau(a^2)/a^2<\infty\) by partial summation. Since

\[ \frac1{4a-1}=\frac1{4a}+O(a^{-2}), \]

Lemma 4.1 gives the first asymptotic, and the asymptotic over the lower half is half of it.

Lemma 4.4 (divisor sum over primes). For \(L\ge3\), \[ \sum_{\substack{a\le L\\4a-1\ \mathrm{prime}}}\frac{\tau(a^2)}a\ll(\log L)^2. \]
Proposition 4.5 (pairwise coprime moduli). Let \(\mathcal B\subset\{a\le L\}\) be such that the integers \(4a-1\) with \(a\in\mathcal B\) are pairwise coprime. Then \[ \sum_{a\in\mathcal B}\frac{|\Lambda_{\mathrm{int}}(a)|}{4a-1}\ll(\log L)^2. \] Thus no subfamily of these congruences with pairwise coprime moduli can carry the cubic mass in Proposition 4.2.

Proof. Split according as \(4a-1\) is prime or composite. The prime part is bounded by Lemma 4.4, because \(|\Lambda_{\mathrm{int}}(a)|\le\tau(a^2)\) and \((4a-1)^{-1}\ll a^{-1}\).

For the composite part, put \(r=P^{-}(4a-1)\). Pairwise coprimality makes these least prime factors distinct. Since \(4a-1\) is composite, \(4a-1\ge r^2\). For every fixed \(\eta_0>0\), \(|\Lambda_{\mathrm{int}}(a)|\le\tau(a^2)\ll_{\eta_0}a^{\eta_0}\), and hence

\[ \frac{|\Lambda_{\mathrm{int}}(a)|}{4a-1}\ll_{\eta_0}r^{-2+2\eta_0}. \]

Choosing \(\eta_0<1/4\) and summing over distinct \(r\) gives a bounded composite contribution.

5 Counting compatible congruences

For each reduced class \(b\pmod{P(z)}\), let \(I_b\) be a finite family of congruences with squarefree moduli \(q_i\le X\), coprime to \(P(z)\), and residues \(c_i\pmod{q_i}\). Write \(w_i=1/q_i\) and \(\mu_b=\sum_iw_i\). Two indices are compatible when their residues agree modulo \((q_i,q_j)\); a set is compatible when every pair is. Put

\[ \begin{gathered} L_S=\operatorname{lcm}(q_i:i\in S),\qquad L_\varnothing=1,\qquad A(S)=\sum_{\substack{i\notin S\\S\cup\{i\}\ \mathrm{compatible}}}w_i(q_i,L_S),\\ F_r=\sum_{\substack{S\subset I_b\\|S|=r\\S\ \mathrm{compatible}}}\frac1{L_S},\qquad F_0=1. \end{gathered} \]

Let \(\Omega_b\) be the finite product probability space over all primes appearing in the moduli \(q_i\). Explicitly,

\[ \Omega_b=\prod_{\ell\mid\prod_{i\in I_b}q_i}\mathbb Z/\ell\mathbb Z, \]

with independent uniform coordinates. The Chinese Remainder Theorem identifies each coordinate assignment at the primes dividing \(q_i\) with a residue modulo \(q_i\). The event \(\mathcal A_i^{(b)}\) is defined by the residue \(c_i\pmod{q_i}\). Thus

\[ \mathbb P(\mathcal A_i^{(b)})=\frac1{q_i}=w_i^{(b)}. \]

If two events are compatible, the Chinese Remainder Theorem gives

\[ \mathbb P(\mathcal A_i^{(b)}\cap \mathcal A_j^{(b)})=\frac1{\operatorname{lcm}(q_i,q_j)}=\frac{(q_i,q_j)}{q_iq_j}; \]

if they are incompatible, the intersection is empty. Two disjoint groups whose moduli are coprime across the groups depend on disjoint coordinates and are independent as families.

Put

\[ W_b=\sum_{i\in I_b}\mathbf 1_{\mathcal A_i^{(b)}}. \]

The Chinese Remainder Theorem gives \(F_r=\mathbb E\binom{W_b}{r}\): an intersection of \(r\) compatible events has probability \(1/L_S\), and an incompatible one is empty.

For distinct events \(i\ne j\), let \(i\sim j\) denote \((q_i,q_j)>1\), and define

\[ \delta_b=\sup_i\sum_{\substack{j\sim i\\j\ne i}}\mathbb P(\mathcal A_j^{(b)}),\qquad \Delta_b=\sum_{\substack{\{i,j\}:i\sim j\\i\ne j}}\mathbb P(\mathcal A_i^{(b)}\cap \mathcal A_j^{(b)}). \]

Fix \(K\ge13\). In this section assume, uniformly in \(b\), that \(\mu_b\to\infty\) as \(X\to\infty\) and

\[ A(S)\le2\mu_b\quad(|S|\le2K\mu_b,\ S\ \mathrm{compatible}),\qquad \delta_b\le\frac14,\qquad \Delta_b=o(\mu_b). \]

Binomial moments

Lemma 5.1 (binomial moments). Uniformly for \(0\le r\le2K\mu_b\), \[ F_r\le\frac{(2\mu_b)^r}{r!}. \]

Proof. The Chinese Remainder Theorem gives \(1/\operatorname{lcm}(L_S,q_i)=w_i(q_i,L_S)/L_S\). Counting the \(r\) choices of an element to delete from a compatible set of size \(r\) gives

\[ rF_r=\sum_{\substack{S\ \mathrm{compatible}\\|S|=r-1}}\frac{A(S)}{L_S}\le2\mu_bF_{r-1}. \]

Induct from \(F_0=1\).

Probability of no event

Theorem 5.2 (probability of no event). Uniformly in \(b\), \[ \mathbb P(W_b=0)\le\exp\{-(1-o(1))\mu_b\}. \]

Proof. We give a conditioning proof related to Spencer's argument. Fix \(b\) and abbreviate \(\mathcal A_i^{(b)}\), \(\mu_b\), \(\delta_b\), \(\Delta_b\) by \(A_i\), \(\mu\), \(\delta\), \(\Delta\). Incompatible neighbours have zero intersection probability and remain adjacent.

Put \(B_S=\bigcap_{j\in S}A_j^c\). If \(\mathbb P(B_{I_b})=0\), there is nothing to prove; otherwise every conditioning event \(B_S\) has positive probability. We first show by induction on \(|S|\) that

\[ \mathbb P(A_i\mid B_S)\le2w_i\qquad(i\notin S). \]

Write \(\Gamma=\{j\in S:j\sim i\}\) and \(T=S\setminus\Gamma\). If \(\Gamma\) is empty, independence gives equality with \(w_i\). Otherwise \(|T|<|S|\), so induction and the union bound give

\[ \mathbb P(B_\Gamma\mid B_T)\ge1-\sum_{j\in\Gamma}\mathbb P(A_j\mid B_T)\ge1-2\delta. \]

Since \(A_i\) is independent of \(B_T\), division by this lower bound proves \(\mathbb P(A_i\mid B_S)\le w_i/(1-2\delta)\le2w_i\).

For a lower bound, the same partition gives

\[ \mathbb P(A_i\mid B_S)\ge\mathbb P(A_i\cap B_\Gamma\mid B_T)\ge w_i-\sum_{j\in\Gamma}\mathbb P(A_i\cap A_j\mid B_T). \]

Fix \(j\in\Gamma\), put \(E=\{k\in T:k\sim j\}\), and write \(T_0=T\setminus E\). No vertex of \(T_0\) is adjacent to either \(i\) or \(j\), so the pair \((A_i,A_j)\) is independent of \(B_{T_0}\). The bound just proved gives \(\mathbb P(B_E\mid B_{T_0})\ge1-2\delta\), and hence

\[ \mathbb P(A_i\cap A_j\mid B_T)\le\frac{\mathbb P(A_i\cap A_j\mid B_{T_0})}{\mathbb P(B_E\mid B_{T_0})}\le\frac{\mathbb P(A_i\cap A_j)}{1-2\delta}. \]

Order the vertices and apply the lower bound with \(S\) equal to the preceding vertices, so that each unordered edge contributes once. The chain rule, \(1-t\le e^{-t}\), and these uniform bounds give

\[ \mathbb P(W_b=0)\le\exp\left\{-\mu+\frac{\Delta}{1-2\delta}\right\}\le\exp\{-\mu+2\Delta\}=\exp\{-(1-o(1))\mu_b\}, \]

uniformly in \(b\).

Finite counting estimate

Theorem 5.4 (finite counting estimate). Fix \(K\ge13\) and put \(R_b=\lfloor K\mu_b\rfloor\). Suppose that \[ P(z)X^{2R_b}\exp\{2\mu_b\}\le N\exp\{-4\mu_b\}. \tag{11} \] Then, uniformly in \(b\), \[ \#\{n\le N:n\equiv b\pmod{P(z)},\quad n\notin \mathcal A_i^{(b)}\ \text{for every }i\in I_b\}\le\frac{N}{P(z)}\exp\{-(1-o(1))\mu_b\}. \]

Proof. Write \(M_b(N)\) for the count in the conclusion and put \(r=2R_b\). Lemma 5.1 gives \(F_s\le(2\mu_b)^s/s!\) for \(s\le r\). For even \(r\), the identity \(\sum_{s=0}^r(-1)^s\binom ws=(-1)^r\binom{w-1}r\) for \(w\ge1\) gives

\[ \mathbf 1_{w=0}\le\sum_{s=0}^r(-1)^s\binom ws\le\mathbf 1_{w=0}+\binom wr. \]

Apply the first inequality to integers in the class \(b\pmod{P(z)}\) and the second in the Chinese Remainder Theorem model. Each compatible intersection has a counting error of absolute value at most \(1\). Thus

\[ \begin{aligned} M_b(N)&\le\frac{N}{P(z)}\{\mathbb P(W_b=0)+F_r\}+\sum_{s=0}^rX^sF_s\\ &\le\frac{N}{P(z)}\left\{\mathbb P(W_b=0)+\frac{(2\mu_b)^r}{r!}\right\}+X^re^{2\mu_b}. \end{aligned} \]

For large \(X\), \(\mu_b\ge2\) and \(r\ge25\mu_b\). The estimate \(\log(r!)\ge\int_1^r\log t\,dt\ge r\log r-r\) implies

\[ \frac{(2\mu_b)^r}{r!}\le(2e\mu_b/r)^r\le(2e/25)^r<2^{-r}\le e^{-4\mu_b}. \]

Condition (11) bounds \(X^re^{2\mu_b}\) by \((N/P(z))e^{-4\mu_b}\). Theorem 5.2 estimates the remaining term and proves the result.

Lifting

Lemma 5.5 (lifting). Let \(\mathcal E\) be a set of positive integers closed under taking divisors. Suppose \(2\le y\ll\log N\) and, uniformly for \(\sqrt N\le T\le N\), \[ \#\{u\le T:u\in\mathcal E,\ (u,P(y))=1\}\le ATe^{-F} \] for \(A>0\) and \(F\ge0\). Then \[ \#(\mathcal E\cap[1,N])\ll AN\log(2y)e^{-F}+N^{3/4+o(1)}. \] The error is uniform for a fixed constant in \(y\ll\log N\).

6 The selected family

Fix \(m\ge4\) for the rest of the proof; all thresholds may depend on \(m\). We take \(X\) large enough that \(z>m\) and put

\[ S_0=X^\eta,\qquad Y_0=X^\lambda,\qquad Y=X^\sigma,\qquad H=X^\theta. \]

Define \(\mathcal F\) to be the set of integers

\[ e=rs^2,\qquad \operatorname{rad}(e)=rs, \]

where \(r\) and \(s\) are squarefree, \((r,s)=1\), \((rs,m)=1\), and

\[ 1\le s\le S_0,\qquad \frac{Y_0}{s}<r\le\frac{H}{s^2}. \]

Here \(\operatorname{rad}(e)\) is the product of the distinct prime factors of \(e\). The ranges give \(e\le H\) and

(13)\[ \operatorname{rad}(e)=rs>Y_0>Y. \]

We first fix a reduced residue class modulo \(P(z)\):

\[ b\in(\mathbb Z/P(z)\mathbb Z)^\times. \]

For \(e\in\mathcal F\), choose a small squarefree odd divisor \(d_-\) and a rough squarefree cofactor \(d_+\) such that

(14)\[ d_-\le U,\qquad d_-\mid P(z),\qquad b\equiv-me\pmod{d_-}, \]
\[ d_-d_+\le Y,\qquad (d_+,P(z))=1,\qquad (d_-d_+,m\operatorname{rad}(e))=1. \]

Finally choose a prime \(p\) satisfying

(16)\[ X^\beta<p\le X^{1-\sigma},\qquad d_-d_+p\equiv-1\pmod{m\operatorname{rad}(e)}. \]

For every quadruple \(i=(e,d_-,d_+,p)\) satisfying these conditions, define

\[ Q_i=d_-d_+p,\qquad q_i=d_+p, \]

and

\[ a_i=\frac{Q_i+1}{m},\qquad \mathcal A_i^{(b)}=\{n:n\equiv-me\pmod{q_i}\},\qquad w_i^{(b)}=\frac1{q_i}. \]

The full modulus is \(Q_i\); after conditioning on \(n\equiv b\pmod{P(z)}\), only the residual modulus \(q_i\) contributes to the density. Indeed, \(d_-\mid P(z)\) and (14) give \(n\equiv-me\pmod{d_-}\). Combining this with \(\mathcal A_i^{(b)}\) gives

\[ n\equiv-me\pmod{Q_i}. \]

The congruence (16) implies that \(a_i\) is an integer and \(\operatorname{rad}(e)\mid a_i\). Because \(e=rs^2\) and \(\operatorname{rad}(e)=rs\), this gives \(e\mid a_i^2\); also \(Q_i=ma_i-1\). Lemma 2.3 therefore gives a representation. Let \(I_b\) be the set of all quadruples above and set

\[ \mu_b=\sum_{i\in I_b}w_i^{(b)}. \]

Since \(d_-d_+\le X^\sigma\) and \(p\le X^{1-\sigma}\), both \(q_i\) and \(Q_i\) are at most \(X\).

Quadratic divisor sums

Proposition 6.2 (quadratic divisor sums). The selected divisor family, before any sieving at small primes, satisfies \[ M_1:=\sum_{e\in\mathcal F}\frac1{\operatorname{rad}(e)}\asymp_m(\log X)^2,\qquad M_\varphi:=\sum_{e\in\mathcal F}\frac1{\varphi(\operatorname{rad}(e))}\asymp_m(\log X)^2. \]

Average over small divisors

Lemma 6.4 (averaged sum over divisors with small prime factors). For \[ A_s:=\sum_{\substack{d\le U\\d\mid P(z)\\d\ \mathrm{odd}\\(d,sm)=1}}\frac1d \] one has \[ \sum_{\substack{s\le S_0\\s\ \mathrm{squarefree}\\(s,m)=1}}\frac{A_s}{s}\asymp_m\log S_0\log z. \]

Proof. Since \(U=z^2\), the upper bound follows from \(A_s\le\sum_{d\le U}d^{-1}\le1+2\log z\) and Lemma 3.3.

Lemma 6.5 (average over small divisors). Uniformly for every \(b\in(\mathbb Z/P(z)\mathbb Z)^\times\), \[ \Sigma_b:=\sum_{\substack{d_-\le U\\d_-\mid P(z)\\d_-\ \mathrm{odd}}}\sum_{\substack{e\in\mathcal F\\b\equiv-me\pmod{d_-}}}\frac1{\varphi(m\operatorname{rad}(e))}\asymp_m(\log X)^2\log z. \]

Proof. Write \(e=rs^2\). For fixed \(s\), observe that a divisor \(d_-\) contributes only when \((d_-,sm)=1\), in which case the congruence is the reduced linear progression \(r\equiv-b(ms^2)^{-1}\pmod{d_-}\). Since \((rs,m)=1\) and \((r,s)=1\), \(\varphi(mrs)=\varphi(m)\varphi(r)\varphi(s)\). By Lemma 6.1,

\[ \sum_{\substack{Y_0/s<r\le H/s^2\\r\ \mathrm{squarefree},\ (r,sm)=1\\b\equiv-mrs^2\pmod{d_-}}}\frac1{\varphi(mrs)}\asymp_m\frac1{sd_-}\log\frac{H}{Y_0s}. \]

Since \(\log(H/(Y_0s))\asymp\log X\) for all \(s\le S_0\), summing over \(d_-\) and using Lemma 6.4 we obtain

\[ \Sigma_b\asymp\log X\sum_{\substack{s\le S_0\\s\ \mathrm{squarefree}\\(s,m)=1}}\frac{A_s}{s}\asymp\log X\log S_0\log z\asymp(\log X)^2\log z. \]

Total event weight

Proposition 6.6 (total event weight). Uniformly in \(b\in(\mathbb Z/P(z)\mathbb Z)^\times\), \[ \mu_b\asymp_m(\log X)^3. \]

Proof. Use \(\mathcal P_X\) and \(G_q(a)\) from Lemma 3.8. Lemma 3.1 gives

(19)\[ \mathcal P_X=\log\frac{1-\sigma}{\beta}+o(1)>0. \]

For every admissible \((e,d_-,d_+)\) the prime condition is the reduced class \(a=-(d_-d_+)^{-1}\pmod{m\operatorname{rad}(e)}\).

Define

\[ V(e,d_-)=\sum_{\substack{d_+\le Y/d_-\\d_+\ \mathrm{squarefree}\\(d_+,P(z))=(d_+,m\operatorname{rad}(e))=1}}\frac1{d_+}. \]

Substituting the discrepancy gives

(20)\[ \mu_b=\mathcal P_X\mathcal M_b+\mathcal G_b, \]

where

\[ \mathcal M_b=\sum_{\substack{d_-\le U\\d_-\mid P(z)\\d_-\ \mathrm{odd}}}\sum_{\substack{e\in\mathcal F\\b\equiv-me\pmod{d_-}}}\frac{V(e,d_-)}{\varphi(m\operatorname{rad}(e))} \]

and \(\mathcal G_b=\sum_{(e,d_-,d_+)}d_+^{-1}G_{m\operatorname{rad}(e)}(a)\), summed over the admissible triples.

For large \(X\), \(X^{\sigma/2}\le Y/d_-\le X^\sigma\) for every \(d_-\le U\). Lemma 3.4, applied with endpoint \(Y/d_-\) and auxiliary modulus \(m\operatorname{rad}(e)\le X^{\theta+1}\), gives \(V(e,d_-)\asymp\log X/\log z\) uniformly in \(e,d_-\) and \(b\). Lemma 6.5 now gives \(\mathcal M_b\asymp(\log X/\log z)\Sigma_b\asymp(\log X)^3\).

The exponents satisfy \(\theta<\nu<\beta/2\). Since \(m\) is fixed, \(mX^\theta\le X^\nu\) beyond a threshold depending on \(m\). Thus every modulus \(m\operatorname{rad}(e)\) is in the range of Lemma 3.8, with a fixed margin at the lower prime endpoint \(X^\beta\).

For a fixed radical \(t=\operatorname{rad}(e)=rs\), the divisor \(s\mid t\) determines \(e=ts\), so there are at most \(\tau(t)\) choices of \(e\). For each such \(e\) there are at most \(U\) choices of \(d_-\), and, by enlarging the range to \(d_+\le Y\) and discarding the extra coprimality restrictions, Lemma 3.4 gives

\[ \sum_{\text{admissible }d_+}\frac1{d_+}\le\sum_{\substack{d_+\le Y\\d_+\ \mathrm{squarefree}\\(d_+,P(z))=1}}\frac1{d_+}\ll\frac{\log X}{\log z}. \]

Taking the maximum discrepancy over reduced residues before summing these multiplicities, and then using \(\tau(t)\le\tau(mt)\) and \(q=mt\), gives

(22)\[ |\mathcal G_b|\ll\frac{U\log X}{\log z}\sum_{q\le X^\nu}\tau(q)\max_{(a,q)=1}|G_q(a)|\ll_m\frac{(\log X)^2}{\log z}=o((\log X)^3). \]

The second bound is Lemma 3.8, since \(U=(\log X)^8\). The maximum over reduced residues makes this error bound independent of \(b\).

Uniformly in \(b\), (22) gives

\[ \frac{|\mathcal G_b|}{\mathcal M_b}\ll_m\frac1{\log X\log z}=o_m(1). \]

Equations (19) and (20) therefore imply

\[ \frac{\mu_b}{\mathcal M_b}=\log\frac{1-\sigma}{\beta}+o_m(1)>0. \]

Together with \(\mathcal M_b\asymp_m(\log X)^3\), this proves the proposition.

Overlap bounds

Proposition 6.7 (event weights in residue classes). Let \(b\in(\mathbb Z/P(z)\mathbb Z)^\times\). Let \(D\le Y\) be squarefree with \((D,P(z))=1\), and let \((c,D)=1\). Define \[ B_{D,c}^{(b)}=\sum_{\substack{i=(e,d_-,d_+,p)\in I_b\\D\mid d_+\\e\equiv c\pmod{D}}}w_i^{(b)}. \] Then, uniformly in \(b,D,c\) in these ranges, \[ B_{D,c}^{(b)}\ll_m\frac{\mu_b}{D^2}. \] Moreover, \[ \sum_{\substack{i=(e,d_-,d_+,p)\in I_b\\D\mid d_+}}w_i^{(b)}\ll_m\frac{\mu_b}{D}. \]
Lemma 6.8 (uniqueness when the large prime is shared). Two distinct compatible events in \(I_b\) cannot share the same large prime \(p\). For any prime \(p>X^\beta\), the total weight of events having this large prime is at most \(H/p\le X^{\theta-\beta}=o(1)\).

Proof. Suppose \((e,d_-,d_+,p)\) and \((f,d'_-,d'_+,p)\) are compatible. Compatibility at \(p\) gives \(me\equiv mf\pmod p\). For large \(X\) we have \(p>m\), so \(e\equiv f\pmod p\). Since \(e,f\le H=X^\theta<X^\beta<p\), we have \(e=f\). The defining congruences then give

\[ d_-d_+p\equiv d'_-d'_+p\equiv-1\pmod{m\operatorname{rad}(e)}. \]

Because \(p>X^\beta>X^\theta\ge\operatorname{rad}(e)\), we have \((p,m\operatorname{rad}(e))=1\), so

\[ d_-d_+\equiv d'_-d'_+\pmod{m\operatorname{rad}(e)}. \]

The products \(d_-d_+\) and \(d'_-d'_+\) are at most \(Y<Y_0<\operatorname{rad}(e)\), so they are equal. Since \(d_-=(d_-d_+,P(z))\), their factorizations agree and the two events are identical.

Lemma 6.9 (overlap bounds). Uniformly in \(b\), \[ \delta_b=o_m(1),\qquad \Delta_b=o_m(\mu_b). \] For every fixed \(K>0\) and every compatible \(S\) with \(|S|\le K\mu_b\), \[ A(S)\le\mu_b(1+o_{m,K}(1)). \]

Proof. The mass of neighbours sharing a medium prime is, by Proposition 6.7, at most

\[ C_m\mu_b\sum_{\ell\mid d_{+,i}}\frac1\ell\le C_m\mu_b\frac{\log Y}{z\log z}\ll_m\frac1{\log\log X}=o_m(1). \]

Together with the direct count in Lemma 6.8, this proves the bound for \(\delta_b\).

For a distinct compatible dependent pair, the common primes are all medium primes, by uniqueness and \(Y<X^\beta\). If \(g=(q_i,q_j)>1\), use \(g\le2\sum_{D\mid g,\ D>1}\varphi(D)\). Since every prime in \(D\) exceeds \(z>m\), compatibility forces \(e_i,e_j\) into the same reduced class modulo \(D\). Thus

\[ \Delta_b\ll\sum_{\substack{1<D\le Y\\D\ \mathrm{squarefree}\\(D,P(z))=1}}\varphi(D)\sum_c(B_{D,c}^{(b)})^2\ll_m\mu_b^2\sum_{n>z}n^{-2}\ll_m\frac{\mu_b^2}{z}=o_m(\mu_b). \]

The middle step uses \(\max_c B_{D,c}^{(b)}\ll_m\mu_b/D^2\) and \(\sum_cB_{D,c}^{(b)}\ll_m\mu_b/D\).

Completion of the proof

Proof of Theorem 2. Fix \(m\ge4\) and \(K=13\). Proposition 6.6 and Lemma 6.9, using the latter with parameter \(2K\), verify the hypotheses of Section 5 for all sufficiently large \(X\), uniformly in \(b\). In particular \(c_m(\log X)^3\le\mu_b\le C_m(\log X)^3\). Choose

\[ \log X=\alpha_m(\log N)^{1/4}, \]

with \(\alpha_m>0\) sufficiently small. Lemma 3.1 gives \(\log P(z)\ll z=(\log X)^4\). For \(R_b=\lfloor K\mu_b\rfloor\),

\[ \log P(z)+2R_b\log X+6\mu_b\ll_m(\log X)^4. \]

Taking \(\alpha_m\) small makes this at most \(\tfrac12\log N\). Since \(\log T\ge\tfrac12\log N\) for \(\sqrt N\le T\le N\), this choice of \(X\), cutoff, and family satisfies (11) throughout that range. Theorem 5.4, summed over the reduced classes modulo \(P(z)\), gives

\[ \#\{n\le T:(n,P(z))=1,\ m/n\text{ has no representation}\}\ll_m T\exp\{-c'_m(\log N)^{3/4}\}. \]

The exceptional set is closed under taking divisors: a representation for a divisor of \(n\) scales to one for \(n\). Apply Lemma 5.5 at the common cutoff \(z=\alpha_m^4\log N\). The factor \(\log(2z)\) and the error \(N^{3/4+o_m(1)}\) are absorbed by decreasing \(c'_m\). Enlarging the implied constant covers the finite remaining range of \(N\).

Proof of Theorem 1. Take \(m=4\) in Theorem 2 and use \(E(N)\le E_4(N)\). The assertion for prime denominators follows as well, since \(\pi(N)\gg N/\log N\) and the factor \(\log N\) is absorbed by decreasing the constant in the exponential.

7 Further remarks

Remark 7.1 (geometry). For numerator \(4\), the surface \(U_n:4xyz=n(xy+xz+yz)\) is an affine part of a Cayley cubic. In projective coordinates \((x_0:x_1:x_2:x_3)\), its closure is \[ 4x_1x_2x_3=nx_0(x_1x_2+x_1x_3+x_2x_3). \] Fixing \(x=an\) gives \((4a-1)yz=an(y+z)\), so the projective closures of these curves are the conic components of explicit plane sections. On the plane \(x_1=anx_0\), the cubic factors as \[ nx_0\bigl((4a-1)x_2x_3-anx_0(x_2+x_3)\bigr)=0. \] The factor \(x_0\) gives the boundary line \(x_0=x_1=0\).
Remark 7.2 (effectivity). The thresholds obtained here inherit the ineffectivity of the usual Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem. Johnston gives effective variants for sifting problems; applying them to this family requires a separate analysis.
